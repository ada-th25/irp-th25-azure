\begin{abstract}
With climate change, there's an increasing need for a higher-resolution wildfire data for better monitoring and risk assessment. GEO satellites have high-temporal resolution, but lacks spatial resolution for a detailed risk assessment. While LEO satellites provide better spatial resolution, but low temporal resolution to monitor rapid wildfire spread. Existing downscaling models have demonstrated their effectiveness in producing high-resolution remote sensing data. But many still fail to capture the fine details and physically plausible outputs. This project aims to achieve a high-resolution spatial downscaling of wildfire data by integrating additional physical covariates to the model to mitigate implausible outputs. The project explores direct (2km to 30m) and cascaded (2km to 375m to 30m) downscaling pipelines and their effectiveness in producing high-resolution wildfire data. The project compares diffusion (EDiffSR), attention U-Net (HAUNet), and transformer-based (ESTNet) downscaling models.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In recent years, due to rising popularity in Net-Zero initiatives, carbon investors are increasingly funding carbon reduction and removal projects, such as reforestation (\cite{InvestmentTrendsOutcomes}). However, these carbon projects are at risk because wildfires can destroy and release the carbon back into the atmosphere. With climate change, wildfires are becoming increasingly severe and frequent (\cite{jonesClimateChangeIncreases,}). This causes economic losses for the insurance industry. For instance, The January 2025 Los Angeles County wildfires set an all-time record, causing \$40 billion in insured losses alone from a single event (\cite{staffHowWildfiresStorms2026}). This project, conducted with Artio, aims to address this gap. By providing highly accurate risk assessment models, this research seeks to give better confidence and enable better risk assessment.

High-spatial and high-temporal resolution wildfire data is favourable in risk assessment. However, high-temporal resolution data obtained from geostationary satellites (GEO) have limited spatial resolution, which makes them difficult to use for accurate risk assessment. While low orbit satellites (LEO) offers high-spatial resolution, but low-temporal resolution.

To bridge this gap, existing methods uses Deep Learning (DL) models for downscaling due to their ability in recognising non-linear spatiotemporal patterns. However, the resolution gap between GEO and LEO is significant (2km - 30m). This increases the risk of producing physically implausible or unrealistic outputs, as a single low-resolution (LR) pixel can correspond to different high-resolution (HR) details (\cite{wangDeepLearningImage2020}). In this project, to mitigate this limitation, the model integrates additional physical information, such as HR topography and landcover to help guide the downscaling process. To further improve downscaling, the project aims to investigate whether cascaded downscaling (2km to 375m to 30m) produces more accurate and better results compared to direct downscaling (2km to 30m). By breaking up the downscaling process, the model trains on a smaller resolution gap, aiming to mitigate physically implausible outputs. This project will compare the cascaded and direct pipeline performance of different downscaling architectures, including hybrid attention U-Net, a transformer-based model, and a diffusion-based model.

\section{Current State of Remote Sensing Downscaling}
Traditional downscaling methods often fail to capture the complex non-linear patterns and variability in remote sensing data. In recent years, DL methods have shown promising results that outpaces traditional approaches (\cite{sdrakaDeepLearningDownscaling2022}). In particular, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and U-Net architectures have been widely used for their ability to capture spatial features. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have also been used for their ability to generate sharp images. Recently, Transformer and Diffusion-based approaches have been proposed for downscaling. Transformers use attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies (\cite{wangVGGTVisualGeometry2025}), while Diffusion models shows good generative ability and fidelity (\cite{wangSinSRDiffusionBasedImage2023}).

However, many of these approaches struggles preserving fine textures and geometric structures (\cite{qiAdvancingImageSuperresolution2026}). This is problematic for wildfire data, where the spatial patterns and textures are crucial for risk assessment. To address these limitations, there is a need for domain-specific architectures that can better capture the characteristics of wildfire data.

Existing literature remains reliant on direct downscaling methods across large resolution-gaps. Some studies have explored cascaded approaches, such as AMCN-Geoi-DBN from \cite{jingCascadedDownscalingCalibration2022}, and CLIT from \cite{chenCascadedLocalImplicit2023}, but there are currently no research from the literature investigating cascaded downscaling in the context of wildfires. The limitations and benefits of cascaded downscaling is also largely unexplored. To address this gap, the project objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop a domain-specific, multi-stage framework for wildfire downsclaing.
    \item Compare the performance of different downscaling architectures in terms of preserving fine textures and geometric structures.
    \item Evaluate the trade-offs of cascaded and direct downscaling and its effectiveness in improving the accuracy of wildfire downscaling.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data and Materials}
Table \ref{tab:data_sources} shows the data sources used for this project. The specifications for the VIIRS, GOES, and Landsat data are outlined in Table \ref{tab:pipeline_configs}.

The data was processed so that all regions of interest (ROIs) are dynamically reprojected to their localised UTM zone based on centroid coordinates. Detections from the NASA VIIRS S-NPP archive were filtered for "nominal" and "high" confidence points. Detections are clustered into $7680 \times 7680$ m grid cells within a 15-minute temporal window of peak activity. VIIRS grid cell pixels record the peak Fire Radiative Power (FRP). Due to extreme class imbalance of the target fire datasets, stratified sampling was applied across temporal extent and fire pixel count bins during data splitting. The closest temporal GOES-16/17 scan within a $\pm 60$-minute guard window was queried. In addition to the native $3.9\ \mu\text{m}$ (Ch. 7) and $11.2\ \mu\text{m}$ (Ch. 14) GOES brightness temperatures, a Thermal Fire Index (TFI) is computed as:

\begin{equation}
    TFI = Ch.7 - Ch.14
    \label{eq:tfi}
\end{equation}

The dataset is split into training/validation/testing (80/10/10). This split utilises stratified random sampling across four quantile bins based on the active fire pixel count per patch ofr balanced representation of fire sizes.

Inputs are scaled prior to model ingestion. The GOES channels undergo Z-score standardisation using mean and standard deviation parameters precomputed exclusively from the training split.

For the targets, the continuous VIIRS FRP values are binarised into active fire masks. Depending on the experimental configuration, thes are subsequently transformed using either spatial dilation via a $3 \times 3$ max pooling operation or conversion into continuous Gaussian heatmaps.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{HAUNet (Hybrid Attention U-Net)}
Among CNN-based approaches, HAUNet (\cite{wangHybridAttentionBasedUShaped2023}) uses hybrid attention to capture both local fine detail and broader contextual structure across multiple scales. HAUNet was selected for its ability to capture local extreme hotspots in near-zero background, which standard U-Nets might smooth out.
HAUNet uses two variants of attention blocks:

\subsubsection{Hybrid Attention Mechanisms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Channel Attention Module (CEM):} Computes channel dependencies with $O(C^2)$ complexity. Given feature map $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, queries $\mathbf{Q}$, keys $\mathbf{K}$, and values $\mathbf{V}$ are generated via $1 \times 1$ convolutions followed by $3 \times 3$ depthwise convolutions. The channel attention map $\mathbf{A}_c$ is derived as:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{A}_c = \text{Softmax}\left(\text{ReLU}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^T}{\alpha}\right)\right) \mathbf{V}
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha$ is a learnable scaling parameter.
    
    \item \textbf{Spatial-Channel Attention (S\_CEM):} Parallel spatial attention branch operating along transposed dimensions $\mathbf{Q}_s, \mathbf{K}_s, \mathbf{V}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N_{head} \times HW \times C}$. The full spatial attention step scales quadratically with spatial resolution as $O((HW)^2)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Full-Resolution Covariate Pyramid Branch}
Because full spatial attention in S\_CEM imposes an $O((HW)^2)$ memory footprint that causes out-of-memory errors on full-resolution inputs, the inputs ($256 \times 256$) are downsampled by factor $D=4$ (i.e. $256/4 = 64$) before passing to the model. 

To prevent losing high-resolution spatial information of the covariates, we introduce a parallel, full-resolution Covariate Pyramid Encoder. This branch processes full-resolution covariate features using Windowed Spatial-Channel Attention Blocks (Windowed-S\_CEM) (\cite{liuSwinTransformerHierarchical2021}) for computational efficiency.

For a feature map partitioned into non-overlapping local windows of size $P \times P$ ($P=8$), Windowed-S\_CEM computes spatial attention independently within each window. The window is shifted sequentially with shift size $P/2$. This keeps the spatial attention complexity to $O(P^4)$ per window, independent of overall spatial input dimensions.

\subsubsection{Multi-Scale Refinement and Feature Fusion}
The main encoder-decoder path produces low-resolution feature representations ($64 \times 64$). The final refinement stage produces the final high-resolution output. At each stage:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Low-resolution features are upsampled via bilinear interpolation followed by $3 \times 3$ convolutions.
    \item Upsampled features are concatenated with matching scale representations extracted from the Covariate Pyramid Encoder.
    \item A $3 \times 3$ fusion convolution maps concatenated features back to channel dimension.
\end{enumerate}
