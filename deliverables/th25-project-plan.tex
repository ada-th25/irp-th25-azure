\begin{abstract}
Wildfires are destructive and a rapidly evolving hazard. With climate change, they become increasingly severe and frequent. This increases the need for a higher-resolution wildfire data for better monitoring and risk assessment. Geostationary satellites (GEO) have high-temporal resolution, but lacks the resolution needed for a detailed wildfire risk assessment. While low orbit satellites (LEO) provide better spatial resolution, but low temporal resolution to monitor rapid wildfire spread. Existing downscaling models have demonstrated their effectiveness in producing high-resolution remote sensing data. But many still suffers from artefacts, blurriness, and hallucinations. This project aims to achieve a high-resolution spatial downscaling of wildfire data by integrating additional physical covariates to the model, such as topography and landcover to mitigate physically implausible outputs. The project compares direct (2km to 30m) and cascaded (2km to 375m to 30m) downscaling pipelines and their effectiveness in producing high-fidelity, high-resolution wildfire data. The project compares diffusion (EDiffSR), attention U-Net (HAUNet), and transformer-based (ESTNet) downscaling models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Description and Motivation}
In recent years, due to rising popularity in Net-Zero initiatives, carbon investors are increasingly funding carbon reduction and removal projects, such as reforestation (\cite{InvestmentTrendsOutcomes}). However, these carbon projects are at risk because wildfires can destroy and release the carbon back into the atmosphere. With climate change, wildfires are becoming increasingly severe and frequent (\cite{jonesClimateChangeIncreases,}). This causes economic losses for the insurance industry. For instance, The January 2025 Los Angeles County wildfires set an all-time record, causing \$40 billion in insured losses alone from a single event (\cite{staffHowWildfiresStorms2026}). This project, conducted with Artio, aims to address this gap. By providing highly accurate risk assessment models, this research seeks to give better confidence and enable better risk assessment.

High-spatial and high-temporal resolution wildfire data is highly desired in risk assessment. However, high-temporal resolution data obtained from geostationary satellites (GEO) have limited spatial resolution, which makes them difficult to use for accurate risk assessment. While low orbit satellites (LEO) offers high-spatial resolution, but low-temporal resolution.

To bridge this gap, existing methods uses Deep Learning (DL) models for downscaling due to their ability in recognising non-linear spatiotemporal patterns. However, the resolution gap between GEO and LEO is significant (2km - 30m). This increases the risk of producing physically implausible or unrealistic outputs, as a single low-resolution (LR) pixel can correspond to different high-resolution (HR) details (\cite{wangDeepLearningImage2020}). In this project, to mitigate this limitation, the model integrates additional physical information, such as HR topography and landcover to help guide the downscaling process. To further improve downscaling, the project aims to investigate whether cascaded downscaling (2km to 375m to 30m) produces more accurate and better results compared to direct downscaling (2km to 30m). By breaking up the downscaling process, the model trains on a smaller resolution gap, aiming to mitigate physically implausible outputs. This project will compare the cascaded and direct pipeline performance of different downscaling architectures, including hybrid attention U-Net, a transformer-based model, and a diffusion-based model.

\section{Literature Review}
\subsection{Current State of Remote Sensing Downscaling}
Traditional downscaling methods often fail to capture the complex non-linear patterns and variability in remote sensing data. In recent years, DL methods have shown promising results that outpaces traditional approaches (\cite{sdrakaDeepLearningDownscaling2022}). In particular, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and U-Net architectures have been widely used for their ability to capture spatial features. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have also been used for their ability to generate sharp images. Recently, Transformer and Diffusion-based approaches have been proposed for downscaling. Transformers use attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies (\cite{wangVGGTVisualGeometry2025}), while Diffusion models shows good generative ability and fidelity (\cite{wangSinSRDiffusionBasedImage2023}).

However, many of these approaches have limitations, particularly in preserving fine-grained textures and geometric structures (\cite{qiAdvancingImageSuperresolution2026}). This is problematic for wildfire data, where the spatial patterns and textures are crucial for accurate risk assessment. To address these limitations, there is a need for domain-specific architectures that can better capture the characteristics of wildfire data.

Furthermore, existing literature remains reliant on direct downscaling methods across large resolution-gaps. Some studies have explored cascaded approaches, such as AMCN-Geoi-DBN from \cite{jingCascadedDownscalingCalibration2022}, and CLIT from \cite{chenCascadedLocalImplicit2023}, but there are currently no research from the literature investigating cascaded downscaling in the context of wildfires. The limitations and benefits of cascaded downscaling is also largely unexplored. To bridge this gap, this project introduces a novel, domain-specific framework, and explores the benefits and limitations of cascaded downscaling.

\subsection{Selected Downscaling Architectures for Comparison} \label{sec:architectures}
\subsubsection{HAUNet}
Among CNN-based approaches, HAUNet (\cite{wangHybridAttentionBasedUShaped2023}) uses hybrid attention to capture both local fine-grained detail and broader contextual structure across multiple scales. This allows it to capture local extreme hotspots in near-zero background, which standard U-nets might smooth out. Therefore, HAUNet will be selected as one of the architectures for comparison, making it a strong baseline for capturing spatially heterogeneous patterns in wildfire data.

\subsubsection{ESTNet}
ESTNet (\cite{kangEfficientSwinTransformer2024}) is a transformer-based architecture that uses efficient spatial-temporal attention to capture long-range dependencies in the data. It is based on Swin-Transformers with group attention. It uses multi-window self-attention for better efficiency without sacrificing reconstruction quality. For transformer-based approach, ESTNet will be selected for comparison. Their ability in capturing long-range dependencies, makes them a suitable candidate for downscaling wildfire data, which has complex spatial patterns. Comparing ESTNet specifically is preferred over alternatives such as SwinIR (\cite{liangSwinIRImageRestoration2021}) because their efficiency could potentially make up for the computational cost in cascaded approaches.

\subsubsection{EDiffSR}
EDiffSR (\cite{xiaoEDiffSREfficientDiffusion2023}) is a conditional diffusion-based model. It uses simplified channel attention for better noise prediction quality, and Conditional Prior Enhancement Module (CPEM) to extract the most valuable information from the LR input. EDiffSR is selected as the diffusion-based model for comparison, because it addresses the problem of one-to-many mapping by effectively leveraging prior information and promotes the condition with more informative input. This makes it a suitable candidate for this project, which has a significant resolution gap and complex spatial patterns.

The architectures (HAUNet, ESTNet, EDiffSR) for comparison is tentatively selected based on their performance in downscaling, and their suitability for the specific requirements of the wildfire data. The reasons for selecting each architecture are outlined in \ref{sec:architectures}. The final selection of architectures and datasets may be adjusted as the project progresses.

\subsection{Datasets}
\subsubsection{Input Datasets}
The input data are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item GOES-17 channel 7 (IR shortwave) of Level 1B (L1B) Radiances product (ABI-L1B-Rad). Has spatial resolution of 2km at every 5 mins. This channel is selected because it is commonly used to identify hot regions (\cite{badhanDeepLearningApproach2024}). This will be the main coarse input.
    \item Copernicus DEM GLO-30: Global 30m Digital Elevation Model with 30m resolution (\cite{europeanspaceagencyCopernicus2022}). DEM band will be used.
    \item ESA WorldCover 10m v100 landcover dataset with 10m resolution. This dataset have 11 land cover classes, including tree cover, shrubland, grassland, and permanent water bodies (\cite{zanagaESAWorldCover102021}).
    \item Other potential covariates include weather data, such as wind speed and direction, temperature, and humidity. The inclusion of these covariates will depend on their availability, relevance and time constraints.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Target Datasets}
The target data are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item  VIIRS (S-NPP) Band Active Fire with 375 m spatial resolution (\cite{earthsciencedatasystemsVIIRSSNPPBand2025}) will be used as the target data for the 2km to 375m stage downscaling. The I-4 channel brightness temperature (3.55-3.93 µm) band will be used.
    \item Landsat-8 active fire dataset includes data for fire scenes taken from August and September 2020 with 30m resolution (\cite{dealmeidapereiraActiveFireDetection2021}). This will be the final target HR target data. Fire-sensitive SWIR and thermal bands will be used.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Design}
The project will evaluate the performance of the selected architectures in both direct and cascaded downscaling pipelines. The direct pipeline will train the model to directly downscale from 2km to 30m resolution. The cascaded pipeline will involve two stages: the first stage downscales from 2km to 375m (trained on GOES and VIIRS data), and the second stage will further downscale from 375m to 30m (trained on VIIRS and Landsat-8 data). The final 30m resolution output from both pipelines will be compared against the Landsat-8 active fire dataset for evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The metrics for evaluation will include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intersection over Union (IoU): Measures the overlap between the predicted fire regions and the actual fire regions in the target data. Higher IoU indicates better performance in capturing the spatial extent of fires.
    \item Intersection's Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (IPSNR): Quantifies the similarity of values in the intersection of the predicted and target fire regions. This is chosen over PSNR because fires only occupy small portion of the image, and PSNR would predict background to be similar (\cite{badhanDeepLearningApproach2024}).
    \item F1 Score / Dice Coefficient: Evaluate similarity or overlap between the predicted and ground truth. They are often used in image segmentation.
\end{itemize}
